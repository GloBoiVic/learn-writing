# Lesson 08: Concrete Prose: Verbs, Nominalizations, Precision, and Jargon

*II. Control sentences · Deliberate Prose, knowledge phase. Read for understanding; practice and testing come later.*

**Concrete prose makes claims that readers can picture, check, and use. Strong verbs help, but the larger goal is to expose actors, actions, quantities, and limits instead of hiding them inside abstractions or specialized language.**

> **Why this matters.** Grant writing needs professional vocabulary, but it also needs a reviewer to understand what the funding will make possible. Specificity is more persuasive than uplift.

## By the end of this lesson

- Spot nominalizations that hide an action or actor.
- Replace vague impact claims with observable particulars.
- Keep necessary technical terms while translating them for an outside reader.

## The map

**claim → actor and action → particulars → limit**

## Core ideas

### Nominalizations

A nominalization turns an action or quality into a noun: *implementation*, *assessment*, or *improvement*. Nouns are not bad. The diagnostic question is whether the noun hides an actor or action the reader needs.

### Specificity

A specific claim names who, what, where, when, how much, or compared with what. “Significant impact” gives the reader a conclusion without the particulars needed to evaluate it.

### Jargon and translation

Retain a technical term when it carries necessary meaning, but define it once or place it beside a plain description. Do not make a reader learn organizational language before receiving the point.

## Worked choices

### Recover the action

**Less controlled**

> Staff conducted an assessment of residents’ needs before the implementation of the pilot.

**More deliberate**

> Staff assessed residents’ needs before launching the pilot.

*Why:* The revision does not ban nouns; it uses verbs where the action is what matters. Keep “assessment” if the assessment itself is the object being compared or evaluated.

### Replace inflated impact

**Less controlled**

> The program had a significant impact on access to services.

**More deliberate**

> The program gave 1,480 residents housing referrals through a verified directory.

*Why:* The second claim is narrower but stronger. A reviewer can ask how the number was counted and what happened after the referral.

## Use this decision rule: Make a claim usable

- Who performs the action?
- What observable thing happened?
- What number, place, timeframe, or comparison makes the claim specific?
- What technical term needs translation?
- What does the evidence not prove?

## Watch for these traps

- Deleting every abstract noun and losing a needed concept.
- Using a synonym to hide repetition without increasing precision.
- Treating “innovative,” “robust,” or “transformative” as evidence.

> **Carry forward.** A concrete sentence is not necessarily simplistic. It is a sentence that gives the reader enough material to understand and evaluate the claim.
