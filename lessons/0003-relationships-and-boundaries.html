I. Build the map

Relationships and Boundaries

Clauses let you show whether ideas are equal, sequential, causal, conditional, contrasting, or concessive. Punctuation and conjunctions should reveal that relationship instead of leaving the reader to guess.

Read for understandingPractice and testing come later
Why this matters. Grant arguments and professional explanations depend on visible relationships: need leads to response, evidence supports a claim, and a condition limits a promise.
By the end of this lesson
  • Distinguish independent and dependent clauses.
  • Choose coordination or subordination based on the relationship you mean.
  • Repair fragments, run-ons, and comma splices by changing the structure deliberately.
The map

Core ideas

Independent and dependent clauses

An independent clause can stand as a sentence. A dependent clause contains a subject-verb core but makes the reader wait for completion. Words such as because, although, when, and if often signal the dependency.

Coordination and subordination

Coordination presents units as peers: “The survey closed, and staff analyzed the results.” Subordination makes one idea the frame: “After the survey closed, staff analyzed the results.” Neither is inherently better; the choice changes emphasis.

Boundaries are meaning

A period separates. A semicolon keeps two independent clauses close without naming the relationship. A coordinating conjunction names a familiar relationship. A dependent clause makes time, cause, condition, contrast, or concession explicit.

Worked choices
One event, three relationships
Less controlled

The survey closed, staff analyzed the results.

More deliberate

After the survey closed, staff analyzed the results.

The original is a comma splice. A period would separate the events; “and” would present them as peers; “after” makes sequence the frame.

A grant qualification
Less controlled

The pilot will expand access. It depends on host sites promoting the kiosks.

More deliberate

The pilot can expand access if host sites promote the kiosks.

The conditional form prevents the second sentence from sounding like an unrelated afterthought or an unconditional promise.

Use this decision rule

Choose the relationship first

  • What is the relationship: addition, contrast, cause, time, condition, or concession?
  • Should the two ideas feel equal, or should one frame the other?
  • Would a reader misread the relationship if the sentence were split?
  • Choose the boundary mark or conjunction that makes the intended relationship visible.
Watch for these traps
  • Fixing a comma splice with a semicolon without considering the relationship.
  • Using “however” as a substitute for a complete sentence structure.
  • Stacking dependent clauses until the main point disappears.
Carry forward. When a sentence feels tangled, do not begin by moving commas. Name the relationship between the ideas, then build the sentence that can carry it.
